## How it works

A pixel-art portrait (`assets/ui/traveller-bust.png`, 47 × 53 texels) sits in a round avatar frame and is lit by one point light that follows the pointer. It is one fullscreen pass (`portrait.wgsl`) and the HDR tail; there is no sprite batcher, camera or depth buffer, because a portrait that never moves does not need them.

1. **Automatic normals.** At load, `normalsFromAlpha` (story 3.3, `src/gpu/lighting/normals.ts`) turns each sprite's alpha into a rounded bevel of `edge radius` texels (default 5, 3.3's default) and a tangent-space normal map, packed as `rgba8unorm` so the shader reads the bytes as written. Nobody painted a normal map; the silhouette is enough for the edges to turn toward the light.
2. **Where things are.** `light.ts` fits the sprite into the largest disc the canvas holds (`fitPortrait`) and keeps the light in _frame units_: the disc's centre is 0 and its radius 1. A resize or a DPR change then moves the light with the portrait instead of making it jump. The shader works in the sprite's texels lifted into 3D as (x, −y, z), so the normal maps (x right, y up, z out) apply as stored; the light's height and range are given in disc radii and converted to texels each frame.
3. **Following the pointer.** With `follow` on `page` (the default) the effect listens on the window, so a small avatar in a header turns toward a cursor anywhere on the page. Inside the disc the light is exactly under the pointer; beyond it, `reachInto` keeps it moving the pointer's way but never more than `reach` radii past the edge, so a cursor at the far side of the page still lights the portrait from that side. The light eases toward its target exponentially (`easeFactor`, rate 7 a second, about a tenth of a second to cover half the distance), scaled by the frame's elapsed time, so the motion does not depend on the frame rate and a frame with no elapsed time leaves the light where it is. When the pointer leaves the window (or the canvas, in `canvas` mode) the light eases onto an idle path, a slow lopsided figure eight over the head (`idleLight`).
4. **Shading.** Per pixel: Lambert times the windowed point-light falloff and a rim, from `src/wgsl/sprite-light-common.wgsl`, the half of 3.3's sprite lighting that needs no batcher (split out for this effect, so a fullscreen pass and the batcher share one copy). The rim comes from the side away from the light and a little behind, a cool kicker on the shadow side as a two-light portrait is lit. Light is rounded into `bands` flat steps. The backdrop is a flat plane a third of a radius behind the sprite, lit by the same light, and the sprite's shadow on it is one texel read: where the ray from the backdrop to the light crosses the sprite's plane, is the sprite opaque?
5. **Palette shading.** With `shading` on `palette` (the default), lighting a colour and snapping the result to the nearest palette colour turned lit skin and the blue backdrop grey, as story 3.5 warned. Instead the light picks a step of a ramp: `shade-table.ts` builds, on the CPU at start-up, a 17 × 48 table that holds for each palette colour and each light level the step of that colour's own ramp (`buildRamp`, with the colour itself put back in) whose OKLab lightness is nearest. The shader finds a texel's row from 3.5's lookup table, whose alpha is the palette index, and its column from the light level. Gain 1 returns the colour exactly as painted; shadows lean cool and highlights warm, as the ramps do. The light's own hue is given up for the ramp's; the `lantern` preset uses plain bands to keep its orange. The rim, a second coloured light, is added afterwards and snapped through the lookup table.
6. **Output.** The scene is written premultiplied with its own alpha and the tail runs with `alpha: 'coverage'`, no bloom, no tonemap and (in palette mode) no output dither, so the corners outside the disc are transparent and palette colours reach the page unchanged.

The inspector shows the albedo, the normals and the light alone, and switches off the light, the rim, the shadow, the bands and the palette one at a time.

On touch, the light follows a finger while it is down (until the browser takes the touch for a scroll) and returns to the idle path when it lifts. Under reduced motion the gallery draws one frame, and the portrait is still.

## Cost

**Study** (`npm run study -- --epic 9`, [docs/studies/epic-9.md](../../../docs/studies/epic-9.md), Apple M1 Max on Dawn, marginal cost per frame with the tail, at the defaults): **0.17 ms at 1920 × 1080 and 0.09 ms at 1266 × 585**. Loads this light sit near Apple Silicon's timing floor, so differences under about half a millisecond are noise; nothing has been measured on an integrated GPU or a phone, where the tier budgets are set.

Not measured on the cost harness: the GPU was shared with other work, so `npm run study` was not run. One reading, taken on a busy GPU and to be read as indicative only: `npm run browser-check` on the Apple M1 Max, 1920 × 1080 with the HUD on, reported a median of 0.53 ms a frame (0.48 to 0.56 over three loads), as the browser measures one frame alone, its timing floor included. The tail's output pass is about half of that.

What it costs: one fullscreen pass that reads a sprite texel twice (colour with the sharp filter, normal by `textureLoad`), one more texel for the shadow, a lookup-table read and a shade-table read per pixel inside the disc; outside it the pass returns at once. Textures are small: the three sprites and their normal maps in two arrays of about 50 kB each, the 32³ lookup table (128 kB) and the shade table (3 kB). The tail's `rgba16float` scene target is most of the texture memory at large sizes. Start-up builds the lookup table (a few milliseconds) and three normal maps (about a millisecond each). Cost scales with canvas pixels; an avatar of 160 CSS pixels at DPR 2 is a twentieth of a 1080p frame.

## Pitfalls

- **Lit-then-snapped colours go grey.** Lighting a colour and taking the nearest palette entry drifts warm skin and saturated blues toward the palette's greys, because a 48-colour palette has few colours between them. The shade table, which only ever walks a colour's own ramp, is what fixed it.
- **Palette shading drops the light's colour.** A ramp's hue is the ramp's. A coloured light (a lantern, a neon sign) needs `bands` shading, or ramps built per light colour.
- **Interior texels are flat.** The automatic bevel rounds only the outer `radius` texels, so a face or a cloak is one flat plane inside its rim and takes one band of light; form comes from the edges. A painted normal map would add detail inside.
- **Page tracking reads layout every frame.** In `page` mode the effect calls `getBoundingClientRect` once a frame to place the pointer relative to the canvas. That is cheap unless the page has invalidated its layout that frame; a page with many avatars could share one listener.
- **A light inside the plane.** The shadow is only cast when the light is in front of the sprite; with a very small `light height` the light skims the surface and the falloff, not the normals, decides the look.
- **Touch has no hover.** A light that waits for the pointer never moves on a phone until it is touched; the idle path is what keeps it alive there.

## When to use it

An avatar, a profile picture or a team card that reacts to the cursor: one small canvas, one pass, safe as decoration on an ordinary page and on phones, over a light or a dark page (the corners are transparent and the backdrop is its own colour). Tier A, provisionally, at about half a millisecond for a full 1080p canvas in Chrome on the M1 Max. For many avatars on one page, draw them into one canvas (one draw per avatar) rather than one WebGPU canvas each. For a sprite in a scene with other lights and shadows, use story 3.3's batcher shade instead; for a still portrait with no pointer, a pre-lit image is cheaper still.
